"Most connected" sounds like a simple contest: count the destinations and crown a winner. In practice there are two quite different questions hiding in it. How many places can you fly to nonstop from this airport? And how many journeys can airlines stitch together through it? A big holiday airport and a big transfer hub can score very differently on each.

This post looks at the current published rankings, explains what hub and point-to-point mean, and turns that into practical advice for picking airports and connections.

## Two ways to measure connectivity

The industry body for European airports, [ACI EUROPE](https://connectivity.aci-europe.org/), publishes an annual Airport Industry Connectivity Report built by SEO Amsterdam Economics. It separates the idea into parts:

- **Direct connectivity:** nonstop destinations from the airport, weighted by frequency. Five daily flights to a city count for more than one weekly flight.
- **Indirect connectivity:** places you can reach from the airport with one connection somewhere else, weighted by how good the connection is (waiting time and detour).
- **Hub connectivity:** how many connecting journeys the airport itself makes possible, for passengers who never leave the terminal.

The data company OAG publishes a different ranking, [Megahubs](https://www.oag.com/megahubs-2026), focused on the hub role. It counts possible connections on the year's busiest day where at least one flight is international and the connection takes place within six hours, then compares that with the number of destinations served.

Different methods give different orders, so it helps to look at both.

### What the rankings tell you

A few patterns stand out:

- **Istanbul leads on almost every measure.** Turkish Airlines built a network that connects Europe, Africa, the Middle East and Asia through one airport, and Istanbul's geography means many of those connections involve little detour.
- **The big four Western European hubs are close together.** Heathrow, Amsterdam, Frankfurt and Paris CDG are each the home base of a major airline group, and each scores high on both nonstop reach and connections.
- **Barcelona is a big airport but a modest hub.** It ranks far lower on OAG's hub index than its passenger numbers suggest, because most people flying there are going to Barcelona, not through it.
- **Low-cost airlines are reshaping direct connectivity.** ACI EUROPE reported that direct connectivity from low-cost carriers was 19% above 2019 levels in 2025, while full-service carriers were 15% below. Among Europe's larger airports, Antalya (+29%), Athens (+24%), Istanbul Sabiha Gökçen (+14%) and Palma de Mallorca (+11%) were well above their 2019 direct connectivity, while Barcelona was only 1% above.

## Hub vs point-to-point, in plain terms

A **hub** is an airport where an airline concentrates flights in waves, so that arrivals from many cities line up with departures to many others. One aircraft from Cagliari to Frankfurt can feed passengers onto dozens of onward flights. The airline does not need enough demand to fill a plane from Cagliari to Chicago; it needs a few passengers from many cities heading the same way.

**Point-to-point** flying links two cities directly, and the airline expects most passengers to start and end their trip at those two airports. Most low-cost carriers work mainly this way, with bases spread across many airports rather than one central hub.

Some airports are both. Heathrow is a hub for British Airways and a destination in its own right for millions of London-bound travellers. Barcelona has a large base for Vueling but most traffic is point-to-point.

Here is how that plays out for you:

| | Big hub | Point-to-point airport |
|---|---|---|
| Nonstop reach | Wide, including long-haul | Often wide in Europe, thin long-haul |
| Connections | Sold on one ticket, with protection if a flight is late | Often separate tickets, at your own risk |
| Timetable | Flights in waves through the day | Often one or two flights a day per route, sometimes seasonal |
| Walking and transfer times | Long distances in very large terminals | Usually smaller, simpler terminals |
| Typical airlines | Full-service network carriers and their alliance partners | Low-cost and leisure airlines |

## What this means when you book

**Fly from the hub when you can.** If you live near a hub, its nonstop reach is your biggest advantage. Our airport maps show every nonstop route from an airport: compare [Frankfurt](/from/FRA), [Munich](/from/MUC) and [Amsterdam](/from/AMS) and you will see how much further a hub reaches than a regional airport. The guide to [how to find direct flights](/blog/how-to-find-direct-flights) shows how to use them.

**Connect on one ticket through a hub.** If you need a connection, a single booking through a major hub usually means the airline must rebook you if the first flight is late, and EU rules measure any [delay compensation](/blog/eu261-flight-delay-compensation) at your final destination. Two separate tickets carry none of that protection.

**Respect the minimum connection time.** Big hubs publish minimum connection times for a reason. Changing terminals at Paris CDG or Heathrow, or passing passport control between Schengen and non-Schengen flights, takes time. A tight connection that is legal on paper can still be stressful in practice.

**Do not assume a hub means the cheapest route.** Hubs concentrate competition on popular long-haul routes, but airlines can also charge more for nonstop flights from their own hub. Compare a nonstop from the hub with a connection via a rival hub before you book. Our comparison of [nonstop vs connecting flights](/blog/nonstop-vs-connecting-flights) goes into the trade-offs.

**Regional airports can surprise you.** Point-to-point airlines often link secondary cities directly, so a small airport near you may have a summer nonstop that the nearest hub does not.

## A note on our own data

Our route maps are mostly built from the OpenFlights route archive, which dates from around 2014, so they show how networks looked then, not today. (Cagliari is the exception: its routes are observed current flights.) In that archive, Paris CDG had the most nonstop destinations of any airport, with 227, followed closely by Istanbul with 226, Frankfurt with 219 and Amsterdam with 205. Barcelona and Munich had around 150 each, and Madrid 137.

Two cautions. First, these are historical counts and not a substitute for the current rankings above. Second, the Istanbul figure in the archive refers to flights from Atatürk Airport, which held the IST code until the new [Istanbul Airport took it over on 6 April 2019](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_Airport). Even a decade ago, the same handful of airports sat at the top. What changed since is the order: Istanbul moved clearly ahead, while several Western European hubs have not regained their pre-pandemic levels. If you are curious why networks shift like that, see [how airlines choose new routes](/blog/how-airlines-choose-new-routes).

## Frequently asked questions

### Which is the most connected airport in Europe?

On the most recent rankings we could find, Istanbul Airport (IST). It was first for direct connectivity in Europe in ACI EUROPE's 2025 report and first worldwide in OAG's Megahubs 2026 index.

### Is Heathrow still Europe's main hub?

It remains one of the top three on both rankings. OAG placed it second in the world in 2026, behind Istanbul, after it had held the top spot the previous year.

### What is the difference between a hub and a busy airport?

Passenger volume measures how many people use an airport. Hub connectivity measures how many onward journeys the airport makes possible for passengers changing planes. Barcelona is very busy but mostly a destination, so it ranks lower as a hub.

### Are connections through a hub safe to book?

On a single ticket, generally yes: the airline is responsible for getting you to your final destination if a delay makes you miss the connection. Leave more time if you need to change terminals or pass passport control.

### Why do your maps show different numbers from the rankings?

Our maps mostly use a route archive from around 2014, which counts nonstop routes listed at the time. The rankings above use current schedules, frequencies and connection quality, so the numbers are not comparable.
