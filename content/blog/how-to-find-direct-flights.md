Most flight searches start with a destination. You type a city, pick dates and scroll through results that mix nonstop flights with itineraries that change planes twice. If what you actually want is to avoid connections, that approach is backwards. The better question is: **where can I go from here without changing planes?** Then pick a destination from that list.

This guide covers how to answer that question for any airport, the traps that make a route look available when it is not, and how to widen your options by looking at more than one airport.

## Step 1: Start from the airport, not the destination

A nonstop route is a property of a pair of airports, so the useful starting point is your departure airport's full list of destinations. There are three good ways to get it.

**A route map.** On this site, every airport has a page at `/from/` plus its three-letter code, for example [London Gatwick](/from/LGW) or [Madrid](/from/MAD). It draws every direct route we have on record as a line from that airport, so you can see at a glance what is reachable and how far away it is. Most of our network comes from an archive dataset from around 2014, so treat it as a map of routes that have existed and are worth checking, not as a live schedule. The one exception is [Cagliari](/from/CAG), where we publish routes we observed operating in the 30 days to 5 October 2026 (see [direct flights from Cagliari](/blog/direct-flights-from-cagliari)).

**The airport's own website.** Most airports publish a "destinations" or "routes" page. These are usually current but often lumped together, with seasonal and year-round routes in one list and no frequency.

**A flexible search tool.** Google Flights has an Explore view that shows a map of destinations from your airport, and a Stops filter where you can choose nonstop only ([Thrifty Traveler](https://thriftytraveler.com/guides/google-flights-explore)). Leave the destination empty, set the filter, and try flexible dates to see the widest set of routes. Explore is built for browsing, with fewer options than the standard search page, so once you have a shortlist, run a normal search for each destination on your real dates.

Using two of these together catches most of what you need: the map shows the shape of the network, and the search tool confirms what is bookable.

## Step 2: Understand airline seasons

The single biggest source of confusion with direct flights is seasonality. Airlines plan their schedules in two IATA seasons. The northern summer season starts on the last Sunday in March and the winter season starts on the last Sunday in October, matching the EU clock changes ([One Mile at a Time](https://onemileatatime.com/insights/iata-summer-winter-airline-schedules/)). In 2026 the switch to winter happens on 25 October.

Many leisure routes, especially to beach and island destinations, only operate in the summer season. Ski routes do the opposite. So:

- A route missing from a search in November might be perfectly real in June.
- A route on an airport's list in September might stop a few weeks later.
- Frequencies change between seasons too. A daily summer route can drop to two flights a week in winter.

When you search, use dates in the season you will actually travel. If you are planning summer in January, set dates in July rather than browsing next week's flights.

Our Cagliari data shows this well. In late September 2026, the busiest domestic routes still had a flight every day, while routes to Gothenburg, Stockholm and several small regional airports showed only a handful of flights in 30 days, typical of summer services in their final weeks.

## Step 3: Look at more than one airport

Big cities usually have more than one airport, and different airlines favour different ones. Low-cost carriers often fly from secondary airports with lower fees, while network airlines concentrate on their hub.

Some examples worth knowing:

| City | Airports to check |
|---|---|
| London | [Heathrow](/from/LHR), [Gatwick](/from/LGW), [Stansted](/from/STN), [Luton](/from/LTN), [London City](/from/LCY) |
| Paris | [Charles de Gaulle](/from/CDG), [Orly](/from/ORY), [Beauvais](/from/BVA) |
| Milan | [Linate](/from/LIN), [Malpensa](/from/MXP), [Bergamo](/from/BGY) |
| Rome | [Fiumicino](/from/FCO), [Ciampino](/from/CIA) |
| Brussels | [Brussels](/from/BRU), [Charleroi](/from/CRL) |

From Cagliari in our 2026 window, for example, Paris Orly had 26 flights in 30 days while Paris Charles de Gaulle had only 4. If you had only searched CDG, you would have concluded that Cagliari to Paris was barely served.

Also widen the circle around your home. A second airport a 90-minute drive away might have a nonstop route your local one does not. Price the drive, parking or train into the comparison, and remember that an early departure from a further airport can mean an extra night or a very early start.

## Step 4: Confirm the route is real

Once you have a candidate, check it before you plan around it.

1. **Search the airline directly.** Route maps and third-party tools lag behind schedule changes. The airline's own booking engine is the authority on whether a flight is on sale.
2. **Check a few weeks of dates.** A route that only shows on Saturdays will look absent if you happen to search a Wednesday.
3. **Read the flight details.** A flight with one flight number can still stop on the way (a "direct" flight with a stop, sometimes called a through flight). Look for "nonstop" or "0 stops" and check the duration looks right for the distance.
4. **Watch for codeshares and wet leases.** The same physical flight can be sold under several airlines' flight numbers (a codeshare), and an airline can hire in another carrier's aircraft and crew to fly its schedule (a wet lease). Our Cagliari records, for instance, show flights on the Rome and Milan routes operated by Fly Air41, a Croatian wet-lease operator, rather than by the airline whose name is on the ticket. Neither is a problem, but they matter for baggage rules, for check-in, and for which airline handles you if something goes wrong. The airline that sold you the ticket is usually your point of contact.

## Step 5: Know when a connection beats a nonstop

Sometimes the honest answer is that there is no direct flight, or that the one that exists is badly timed or expensive. A well-timed connection on a single ticket can be the better option, especially on long trips. Our article on [nonstop vs connecting flights](/blog/nonstop-vs-connecting-flights) covers how to compare total travel time, misconnection risk and your rights if something goes wrong.

If you are curious why some routes exist and others do not, [how airlines choose new routes](/blog/how-airlines-choose-new-routes) explains the economics, and [the A321XLR and long thin routes](/blog/a321xlr-long-thin-routes) covers the aircraft opening up smaller nonstop markets.

## A worked example

Say you live near Bologna and want a long weekend somewhere warm in October without changing planes.

1. Open the [Bologna](/from/BLQ) route map and note the southern destinations: Spain, Portugal, Greece, the Italian islands, North Africa.
2. In a search tool, set Bologna as origin, leave the destination open, select nonstop only and pick a flexible weekend in October.
3. Compare what appears with the map. Routes on the map but missing from the search may be summer-only or discontinued. Routes in the search but missing from the map are likely newer than our archive.
4. Repeat for [Bergamo](/from/BGY) or [Pisa](/from/PSA) if you are willing to travel for a better schedule.
5. For your shortlist, check the airline's own site for exact days and times.

That process takes about fifteen minutes and gives you a far better view of your options than typing in one destination at a time.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a route map as a timetable.** Maps (ours included) show where airlines fly or have flown. Always confirm dates.
- **Searching the wrong season.** Browsing next week to plan next summer hides most seasonal routes.
- **Ignoring secondary airports.** In many European cities the low-cost network sits away from the main hub.
- **Assuming "direct" means nonstop.** Check the number of stops, not just the flight number.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a direct and a nonstop flight?

A nonstop flight goes from takeoff to touchdown at your destination with no stops. A "direct" flight keeps the same flight number but may stop on the way, sometimes letting passengers on or off. In everyday use many sites use "direct" to mean nonstop, so check the number of stops.

### Why does a route appear on a map but not in flight search?

Usually because it is seasonal and your dates fall outside the season, because it does not run on the day you searched, or because it has been discontinued. Our maps for airports other than Cagliari come from an older archive, so some routes no longer operate.

### When do summer routes start and stop?

The IATA summer season begins on the last Sunday in March and ends when the winter season starts on the last Sunday in October. Individual routes can start later or end earlier within that window.

### Is it worth driving to another airport for a nonstop flight?

Often, if the alternative is a connection. Add up the drive, parking or train fare and the time, then compare it with the total journey time and risk of a connecting itinerary.

### How do I find all destinations from one airport?

Use an airport route map like the ones on this site, the airport's own destinations page, and a flexible search tool with a nonstop filter and an open destination. Then confirm on the airline's site.
